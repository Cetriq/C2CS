Code to Common Semantics — an open standard in the making

Software is no longer scarce.
Understanding is.

AI can now write more code than any team can read. The question that decides whether you can trust that code is no longer does it work? — it is what does it actually do?

C2CS is an open, in-progress standard for answering that question: a machine-readable way to state what a piece of software may do, record what it appears to do and what it was seen doing — and check whether the three agree.

The problem, in plain words

Picture a service your organization runs. An AI wrote most of it. It passes its tests. It works. And one Tuesday it quietly starts sending data to a telemetry vendor nobody ever approved.

Nothing "broke". No test failed. The behavior was simply never part of what anyone decided — and there was no place where that decision lived, so there was nothing to check the running system against. Today, you find this in an incident review. If ever.

The supply-chain world has learned to describe two of the three things that matter about software:

SBOMwhat the software consists ofstandardized
SLSAhow the software was builtstandardized
?what the software doesmissing

That third row is what C2CS is for. Not documentation that goes stale — a machine-checkable statement of behavior.

The idea: three documents, honestly separated

Think of it like a rental agreement for software behavior. The lease says what is allowed. Inspections record what was observed. And a report says whether they agree. C2CS keeps these three strictly apart, because they are three different kinds of statement:

Contract

what shall hold

Written and approved by people, kept in the repo, reviewed like code. It declares capabilities ("may talk to the internal database"), prohibitions ("must never send data outside the internal zone — and here is why"), and the business meaning: operations, data, classifications.

Assessment

what it appears to do · what it was seen doing

Produced by tools. Either inferred from the code — a hypothesis with a stated confidence — or observed at runtime, with evidence. A tool that couldn't analyze something says unknown. It never bluffs.

Verdict

do they agree?

A deterministic comparison of contract against observations: confirmed, not observed, drift (undeclared behavior), or violation (a prohibition was broken). Same inputs, same verdict — from any conforming tool.

What it looks like

A contract fragment — the prohibition that would have caught the Tuesday incident:

forbidden:
  - id: forb.net.external
    category: network
    match: {direction: outbound, host: any, except: ["*.internal.acme.example"]}
    rationale: Customer data must not leave the internal zone.
    by: claes@acme.example

And the verdict, after a runtime observation caught the undeclared connection:

results:
  forbidden:
    - claim: forb.net.external
      verdict: violation
      matched: ["sha256:aa11…#obs.net.9"]   # traceable to the exact evidence
overall:
  outcome: not-conformant

And for AI agents, it becomes an answer

A coding agent connected to the model (via MCP) doesn't have to guess what your system is allowed to do. It asks: 

> May this service call api.riskvendor.example?

FORBIDDEN by forb.net.external
  — rationale: Customer data must not leave the internal zone.
Do not implement this without a contract change approved by a human.

The agent gets the why, not just the rule — and the change request goes to a person, not into production.

Why you can take this seriously

Every security pitch claims too much. This one is built around what cannot be done:

  • We do not claim to divine intent from arbitrary code. That is mathematically impossible (Rice's theorem), and any tool promising it is wrong. C2CS splits the problem instead: inferring what code probably does is best-effort and clearly labeled as hypothesis; checking observed behavior against a declared contract is tractable engineering — and that is the part that gets verified.
  • "We don't know" is a first-class answer. A category a tool didn't analyze is unknown — never silently empty. An unknown can make a verdict worse, never better. No false green lights.
  • The rejections are published. The vocabulary registry documents what was refused and why — categories, relations, classifications that seemed reasonable on the drawing board but failed the admission tests. Words earn their place through use, not through plausibility.
  • Conformance is defined by fixtures, not by our tools. Anyone can build an independent implementation from the published schemas, registry, and test fixtures alone — and prove it behaves identically. Our own reference tools have no special status.
  • The model's limits are documented by us, first. Our own boundary test shows where the approach thins out (systems that do all their work through one cloud API), published in the spec rather than discovered by critics.

Where it stands today

Honest status, updated as it changes. This is early-stage work.

Exists and works

  • The specification: schema, 14 argued design decisions, governance
  • The vocabulary registry: four effect categories with precise matching rules
  • A conformance suite: 70+ fixtures any implementation can test against
  • c2cs-extract — a .NET analyzer that reads a compiled binary and drafts claims, and refuses to guess when values are dynamic
  • c2cs-serve — an MCP server that lets an AI agent ask "may I do X?" and get the contract's answer

Doesn't exist yet

  • A runtime observation harness (example verdicts are hand-computed)
  • A verification engine implementing the full verdict semantics
  • Signed attestations in practice
  • Extractors for languages beyond .NET
  • External implementations and users — the thing that makes a standard a standard. That could be you.

The manifesto

Software is no longer scarce. Understanding is.

The age of AI changes the bottleneck of software engineering. We no longer struggle to produce code. We struggle to understand, verify, and govern it.

We believe software needs a shared semantic representation — not just for humans, but for AI. We believe intent should be declared and approved by people, behavior should be observed by tools, and the comparison between them should be deterministic and open.

We believe a standard must be honest about what cannot be known: unknown is information, hypotheses are not facts, and no signature makes a statement true — it only proves who made it.

And we believe none of this should belong to any single vendor. The words that describe software behavior should mean the same thing everywhere, forever.

Argue with us

This is a research initiative becoming a standard, not a finished product — and the design is meant to be argued with. Three invitations:

  • Tool builders: the conformance suite lets you build an extractor, validator, or verifier for your language and prove it conforms — without reading a line of our code. The first external implementation is the moment this stops being our opinion and becomes an agreement.
  • Security & GRC people: read the compliance walkthrough — policy → approval → observation → evidence in one document chain — and tell us where it breaks for your reality.
  • Researchers: the whitepaper carries the open question: can LLM-inferred semantics produce meaningfully tighter least-privilege policy than behavioral traces? The confidence model is designed to be measured against verdicts. Help us measure it.

Everything happens in the open: github.com/Cetriq/C2CS — issues welcome, changes via public RFC process.
